Zero-noise extrapolation fits a noise-scaling curve to measurements taken at amplified noise and evaluates it at zero. Implementations are commonly validated by holding out one amplified-noise point and checking the fit predicts it. We show this validation is systematically blind to the failure mode that matters, and quantify the gap. In a per-Pauli extrapolation over 756 curves, the held-out prediction error was 7.5 x 10^-4 on quantities bounded to [-1,1] -- an excellent fit by any conventional standard -- while the same fits, evaluated at the extrapolation target, turned a 0.0652 kcal/mol energy error into 33.48 kcal/mol, an amplification of 513 x . The input carried no device noise: it was multinomial shot noise drawn from exact statevector probabilities for a noiseless model, so the entire discrepancy was manufactured by the estimator. We trace the mechanism to the variance inflation factor of the fit evaluated off its data support. At the held-out point this factor is below unity for three of the four model classes used; at the extrapolation target it reaches 4.0, 6.9 x 10^7 and 1.7 x 10^20 for the quadratic, exponential and rational classes. Shot noise then drives model-class selection toward the flexible nonlinear families -- chosen for 62% of curves -- precisely because they interpolate best, and those are the classes whose extrapolation is worst conditioned. A layer-by-layer control shows circuits, measurement, reconstruction and the extrapolator's own algebra are each exact to 10^-11 or better on noiseless input, isolating the pathology to the interaction of finite sampling with off-support evaluation. We recommend that any zero-noise extrapolation report its variance inflation at the evaluation point, and that validation be performed by extrapolating to a withheld lower-noise point rather than by interpolation.
